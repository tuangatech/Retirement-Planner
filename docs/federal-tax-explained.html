Retirement Planner · Tax Model Explainer

Federal tax for retirees

Why Social Security is never fully taxed but is rarely fully exempt either, why the tax on your next withdrawal dollar can briefly run at nearly twice your headline rate, and how this simulator turns IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 into a single number.

Every state this simulator models eventually hands off to one shared federal calculation. Unlike a state's flat or bracketed rate, the interesting part of the federal model isn't the rate — it's what counts as taxable in the first place. Social Security is taxed by a formula, not a percentage, and that formula interacts with everything else you draw in a way that produces a genuine "torpedo": a stretch of income where tax rises faster than the headline rate would suggest.

The whole model in one line

Federal tax = 12% (your rate) × max(0, [taxable Social Security + ordinary income] − standard deduction)

PART ONE · THE TAXHow income becomes taxable federally

Federal tax has no state-style "exclusion." Instead it runs a formula to decide how much of your Social Security counts at all, adds that to everything else, and shields the total with a standard deduction before applying one flat marginal rate:

Step 1 — how much of Social Security counts

The IRS provisional-income formula (IRC §86) is not a fixed percentage. It compares provisional income — every other AGI item plus half your Social Security benefit — against two thresholds, and phases SS into the taxable base in two tiers:

Provisional-income thresholds (frozen since 1983/1993 — not inflation-indexed)
Filing status0% tier ends50% tier endsAbove the second threshold
Single$25,000$34,00085% of the marginal dollar
Married filing jointly$32,000$44,00085% of the marginal dollar

The result is capped at 85% of the benefit — Congress never lets more than that become taxable, no matter how high other income runs. This part of the model is the real statutory formula, not an approximation; the freeze on the thresholds is what makes it bite harder every year (Part Two).

Step 2 — the standard-deduction floor

Taxable Social Security plus every other ordinary item (pensions, part-time work, rental, tax-deferred withdrawals, brokerage gain, non-medical HSA withdrawals after 65) forms the taxable base. The standard deduction is subtracted from that base before any rate applies — base amount, plus an age-65 addition, plus the temporary OBBBA senior bonus:

Standard deduction components, TY2026
ComponentSingleMFJNotes
Base standard deduction$16,100$32,200Inflation-indexed each year
Age-65+ addition$2,050$1,650 / spousePer person once that person turns 65
OBBBA senior bonus (2025–2028)$6,000 / person$6,000 / personSunsets after TY2028 — see Part Three

Step 3 — one flat rate above the floor

Whatever remains after the deduction is taxed at a single user-set rate — 12% by default (combinedEffectiveRate in TaxSettings, editable on the wizard's Assumptions step). When a modeled state is selected this rate is federal-only, since state tax is computed separately from its own rules; for any other state it doubles as the user's own federal + state blend. Because the deduction and the SS formula are both modeled explicitly, this is closer to a marginal rate than a blended effective one — see Part Three for why it stops there and doesn't become a full bracket table.

PART TWO · THE SHAPEWhy the marginal rate can spike above the headline

Because taxable Social Security rises with other income up to the 85% cap, every extra dollar of withdrawal does double duty while the formula is active: it adds itself to the base and pulls up to 85 more cents of Social Security in behind it. The result is a marginal rate on your next dollar that can run at up to 1.85× the headline rate — not hypothetically; it's the direct algebra of the 85%-tier formula.

The trap

The torpedo band doesn't exist at all before you claim Social Security — with no SS benefit, ordinary withdrawals are simply taxed at the flat rate above the deduction floor, full stop. The same nominal withdrawal that cost 12% the year before claiming can cost up to 22.2% the year after, purely from this interaction — no bracket changed, no extra dollar of spending happened. The engine's tax-smart withdrawal solver re-derives the correct tax-free room every single year (Part Four), so the plan itself doesn't get this wrong — but it does mean a plan's "tax-free withdrawal room" can shrink sharply the year Social Security starts, which is worth knowing before you're surprised by it.

PART THREE · SCHEDULED & SKIPPEDWhat's modeled, what sunsets, and what we skip

PART FOUR · IMPLEMENTATIONHow the simulator computes it

The tax rules above are the interesting part. This section is a short tour of where they live in the code, for anyone reading the engine.

Constants live directly in TypeScript, duplicated by hand in Python

Unlike per-state rules, federal constants aren't split into a shared JSON file — there are only two filing statuses to cover, not fifty states, so a JSON-plus-reader layer buys less. They sit in TAX_RULES in src/lib/calculations/taxes.ts, with the primary source cited beside each one, and are duplicated by hand into scripts/verify_plan.py's own TAX_RULES table — the same independent-re-derivation pattern the state modules use, just without the shared file.

The tax-free withdrawal room is bisected against the real formula

To know how much can be pulled from a tax-deferred account before the torpedo band starts, the engine can't just subtract: each candidate withdrawal changes provisional income, which changes how much Social Security joins the base, which changes the total — the same circular problem Part Two describes. Rather than solve that algebraically, it bisects against the actual provisional-income formula:

// taxes.ts — calculateTaxFreeTaxDeferredRoom bisects against the real §86 formula
const totalTaxableAt = (x) =>
    calculateTaxableSocialSecurity(ssBenefit, otherOrdinaryTaxable + x, filingStatus, ssTaxablePctCap)
    + otherOrdinaryTaxable + x;
// bisect for the largest x with totalTaxableAt(x) <= deduction

This is the same discipline as Georgia's marginal-rate handling in state-tax-explained-georgia.html: hand the solver the real formula rather than a probed or assumed rate, and the torpedo's kinks come out correctly for free.

Simplifications, stated plainly

  • One flat marginal rate above the floor in the multi-year engine — no 10–37% bracket table. User-set, default 12%. The standalone comparison tool now differs here (Part Three).
  • No 0%/15%/20% long-term capital-gains preference, anywhere in the app — brokerage gains are taxed as ordinary income everywhere, including the comparison tool.
  • Provisional-income thresholds are never inflated — correct, since they are statutorily frozen. This is precisely what makes the torpedo band swallow more retirees every year as nominal incomes rise with inflation.
  • OBBBA senior-bonus MAGI phase-out not modeled — negligible above $150k MFJ / $75k single, rare for this tool's audience.
  • No itemized deductions, above-the-line adjustments, or credits.
  • No survivor's penalty yet — the MFJ → single transition on the first spouse's death (deduction roughly halves, brackets compress) is Phase 3, deferred. See docs/2-federal-tax-model.md.
Verification

Federal arithmetic is re-derived independently in Python (verify_plan.py) and cross-checked on exported runs — the most recent export checked, a 10,000-run plan with a 33% success rate, passed 528 of 528 deterministic checks across all 33 simulated years at the p50 percentile.
