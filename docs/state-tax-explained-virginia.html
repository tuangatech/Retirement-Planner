Retirement Planner · Tax Model Explainer

Virginia state tax for retirees

Virginia's one retiree benefit is means-tested, and the way it is taken away creates a band where every extra dollar you withdraw is taxed at double the headline rate. That single quirk changed how this simulator sizes withdrawals.

Georgia hands retirees a large exclusion that grows with their retirement income. Virginia does close to the opposite: one deduction, available only from age 65, worth $12,000 a person — and it shrinks dollar-for-dollar once household income passes $50,000. Social Security is exempt, as in Georgia. There is no pension or 401(k) exclusion of any kind. Virginia is simply a state where a comfortable retiree pays real tax, every year.

The whole model in one line

VA tax = brackets( max(0, [federal AGI − taxable Social Security] − age deduction − standard deduction − personal exemptions) )

PART ONE · THE TAXFour subtractions, then a bracket schedule

Virginia starts from your federal adjusted gross income, removes the Social Security that was federally taxable, then subtracts three more things before applying rates. Here is a single 67-year-old with $60,000 of income — deep enough into the phase-out to show what it does:

The brackets

Unlike New York's and California's, Virginia's rate schedule does not vary by filing status — single and married filers use the same one:

Because the top bracket starts so low, any retiree with meaningful income lands in it, and the whole schedule collapses to a tidy shortcut: tax = 5.75% × taxable − $257.50. The simulator implements the real four-step walk anyway — it is four lines, and a modest retiree drawing $30,000 genuinely sits in the 5% bracket, where the shortcut would be wrong.

Below $11,950, no tax at all. Virginia imposes no tax and requires no return if Virginia AGI is under $11,950 (single) or $23,900 (married) — § 58.1-321. That threshold sits above the deduction plus exemption ($9,680 for a single filer), so without modelling it the simulator would bill a low-income retiree tax they do not owe. It matters more after 2030, when the standard deduction drops and that gap widens.

PART TWO · THE SHAPEThe band where your marginal rate doubles

This is the one thing worth understanding about Virginia if you are planning withdrawals.

The age deduction disappears at $1 per $1 of income above the threshold. So inside that band, a dollar withdrawn does two things: it adds a dollar of income, and it destroys a dollar of deduction. Taxable income rises by two dollars, and the effective marginal rate is 2 × 5.75% = 11.5%.

The band is only $12,000 wide — $50,000 to $62,000 for a single 65-year-old, $75,000 to $99,000 for a couple both over 65. A normal year's withdrawal walks straight through it.

Planning consequence

A Virginia retiree in the band faces a combined federal + state marginal rate that is genuinely punishing, and it is worst in precisely the income range most middle-class retirees occupy. Stacked on the Social Security "tax torpedo" — where each withdrawn dollar also drags up to 85¢ of benefits into federal tax — the true cost of the next dollar can exceed 30% for someone whose headline federal bracket is 12%.

PART THREE · SCHEDULED CHANGESThe 2030 cliff we model and don't believe

Virginia's standard deduction is on a statutory schedule that reverts sharply in 2030 — from $9,300 back to $3,000 for a single filer. This is what current law says.

Why not just assume the extension? The project's rule is to model what is enacted and unconditional, and to freeze anything contingent. Virginia's reversion is unconditional — it happens unless a future legislature acts — so it is modelled. Georgia's further rate cuts are contingent on revenue triggers, so they are frozen. Both choices push the estimate the same way: toward slightly more tax, never less. The alternative is forecasting legislatures, which this tool refuses to do.

PART FOUR · IMPLEMENTATIONWhat Virginia forced us to change

Georgia's flat rate let the engine take a shortcut. Virginia's phase-out proved the shortcut wrong, and fixing it is the most consequential change in this work.

The shortcut, and why it broke

To withdraw enough from an account to cover the tax the withdrawal itself causes, the engine must solve gross − tax(gross) = need. Until now the state half of that used a single rate, measured with a probe withdrawal: multiply the draw by it and you have the state tax. For Georgia's flat 4.99% that is essentially exact.

For Virginia it cannot be. A single rate has to be either the in-band 11.5% or the out-of-band 5.75%, and a real draw crosses the boundary:

The fix: pass the formula, not a number

The withdrawal engine no longer takes a state rate. It takes a function — the state's own tax calculation — and evaluates it at whatever draw the solver is currently testing. The curve then bends wherever the law bends, with no per-state algebra anywhere in the withdrawal code:

// yearlyProjection.ts — what gets handed to executeWithdrawals
(draws) => computeStateTax(stateRules, { ...stateInputsBase,
              taxDeferredWithdrawals: draws }).tax - initialStateTax

// withdrawals.ts — federal and state, both re-run their real formula
const taxOnDraws = (draws) =>
    (federalTaxableWith(draws) - federalTaxableBeforeDraws) * effectiveTaxRate
    + stateTaxOnDraws(draws);

This deleted more code than it added: the probe-based marginal-rate function is gone entirely, because nothing needs a single rate any more.

Measured effect

Across a 60-seed sweep of the default plan, phantom spending — dollars the plan spent without withdrawing them — fell from $12 per run to $0.52 in Georgia, with the worst single year going from −$362 to −$0.21. Virginia, carrying roughly nine times Georgia's tax bill, leaks the same sub-dollar rounding. In other words the state gross-up is now exact, where it used to be an estimate with a disclosed residual.

Where the constants live

Every Virginia number sits in src/lib/calculations/stateTaxRules.json against a link to the Code of Virginia section or the Form 760 worksheet that states it. That file is JSON rather than TypeScript so the independent Python verifier, scripts/verify_plan.py, reads the same constants instead of re-declaring them — one file, two readers. The formulas stay separately implemented in each language, which is the whole point: a wrong constant is caught by human review against the primary source, a wrong formula by the verifier disagreeing with the engine.

What is deliberately not modelled

  • The 2030 reversion is treated as real — likely overstating Virginia tax from 2030 on, as described above.
  • The separate $12,000 deduction for taxpayers born on or before 1 January 1939. They are 87+ in 2026, outside this tool's audience, and it would need a birth year rather than an age.
  • Blindness exemptions, the military benefits subtraction, dependent exemptions, and federal conformity adjustments.
  • Brackets and exemptions are never inflated — correct, since Virginia indexes neither. A deliberate asymmetry with the federal deduction, which is indexed, and one that quietly raises real tax every year.
  • Localities levy no income tax in Virginia, so there is nothing to add.

One structural piece of luck. Virginia's age deduction is means-tested per return on combined income against a pooled cap. This simulator already pools a couple's accounts and cannot attribute a withdrawal to one spouse — which for Georgia's per-person exclusion is an approximation that over-excludes lopsided couples. For Virginia it is not an approximation at all: pooling is exactly what the statute does.
