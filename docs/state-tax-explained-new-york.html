Retirement Planner · Tax Model Explainer

New York state tax for retirees

New York taxes a government pension and a private one completely differently — one is fully exempt at any age, the other gets a capped exclusion only once you're near 60. And above an entirely ordinary retirement income, New York claws back the value of its own lower tax brackets, in a pattern that looks like a rate roller coaster but is really five overlapping recapture bands resolved into one bracket table.

Georgia excludes retirement income by age. Virginia gives a means-tested age deduction. California gives no retiree benefit at all but taxes a credit off the bill instead of off taxable income. New York does something none of the three do: it taxes the same dollar of pension income differently depending on where it came from. And its complications don't stop at retirees — a benefit-recapture surtax reaches ordinary earners too, not just the ultra-wealthy the way California's does.

The whole model in one line

NY tax = brackets([federal AGI − taxable Social Security] − government pension (all of it) − min($20,000 × eligible people, private pension + IRA/401(k) withdrawals) − standard deduction)

PART ONE · THE BENEFITTwo pensions, two completely different rules

New York starts like every other modeled state: federal AGI, minus the Social Security that was federally taxable, minus a standard deduction. Where it diverges is the very next line. Every other modeled state applies one retirement benefit to all retirement income alike. New York applies two unrelated ones, chosen entirely by the pension's source:

  • Government pensions — from New York State, its local governments, the federal government, or the military — are subtracted from AGI in full, at any age, with no dollar cap (Tax Law §612(c)(3)).
  • Everything else — private-employer pensions, annuities, and IRA/401(k) withdrawals — gets a $20,000-per-person exclusion, and only once the person is old enough (§612(c)(3-a)).

Before 60, the private exclusion is $0 — not partial. The same $30,000 private pension at 59 owes tax on $22,000 (only the standard deduction shields it): (30,000 − 8,000) × brackets = $1,023.00, more than thirteen times the age-60 bill. Nothing else this tool models has that sharp an age cliff on ordinary pension income — Georgia's exclusion at least starts partial at 62.

PART TWO · THE FINE PRINTNot poolable, and a birthday the engine can't see

Per person, and a couple can't share

The $20,000 exclusion belongs to the person who earned the pension. IT-201-I's own instructions are explicit that a couple cannot combine or reassign it:

"If you both qualify, you and your spouse can each subtract up to $20,000 of your own pension and annuity income. However, neither of you can claim any unused part of your spouse's exclusion."

The same shape of simplification already exists for Georgia: this simulator's accounts are pooled, with no attribution of a specific withdrawal to a specific spouse, so a couple whose private pension/IRA income is lopsided toward one spouse can be over- or under-excluded relative to what a real, per-spouse return would compute:

The exclusion starts at 60, not the statutory 59½

New York's own threshold is a half-year: "if you were age 59½ or older for the entire tax year." This simulator's engine has no such thing — every simulated year is a whole calendar-year age, one age per year, with no fractional precision anywhere else in the model either. Rounding the threshold up to 60 denies the exclusion for the half-year someone is actually eligible, which understates the exclusion and overstates tax — deliberately, since every other place this project takes a shortcut errs in the same conservative direction.

PART THREE · THE RATE ROLLER COASTERNew York claws back its own tax break, and it starts at an ordinary income

California's surtax is a single, sharp kink at $1,000,000 — reachable only at the very top of the income distribution. New York's is stranger and, for this tool's audience, far more consequential: above $107,650 of combined income — not doubled for joint filers — New York recaptures the value of its own lower brackets in a series of $50,000-wide bands, and $107,650 is an entirely ordinary New York retirement income.

Not a rare edge case

California's $1,000,000 surtax line and New York's own top bracket are both genuinely out of reach for this tool's audience. $107,650 of combined household income is not — two modest pensions and a Social Security benefit can clear it. This is the one modeled-state discontinuity that a typical plan can actually hit.

Why the rate moves like this: five recaptures, not one

New York's brackets, as published, are an ordinary nine-rate schedule: 4%, 4.5%, 5.25%, 5.5%, 6%, 6.85%, 9.65%, 10.3%, 10.9% — the numbers most articles quote. But that schedule only applies below $107,650 of NY AGI. Above it, New York adds a supplemental tax that phases in the difference between the published rate and the top rate a filer's bracket would otherwise imply, over a series of $50,000-wide bands — once for the ordinary "millionaire's tax" mechanism that predates 2026, and again, freshly, for the 2026 middle-class rate cut itself (a targeted tax cut that phases back out for anyone whose income is high enough that the cut wasn't meant for them). Layered together, the two recaptures are what produce six rate changes across a $185,000-wide span instead of one.

PART FOUR · WHERE THE NUMBERS CAME FROMA withholding table did the hard part first

Reimplementing New York's recapture the way its own tax-return worksheets describe it — five separate worksheets, each testing a different income breakpoint and phasing in its own band — would have meant a genuinely new engine mechanism. It turned out to be unnecessary. New York's own payroll department publishes an official withholding schedule every January (NYS-50-T-NYS), and its "Method II Exact Calculation" tables have already done the work of flattening the recapture into an ordinary, cumulative bracket table — confirmed by checking that every row's stated dollar base equals the running total of every row before it, the same convention Virginia's plain bracket table already uses. The bracket table above is that table, transcribed directly, nothing smoothed or re-derived.

"The 2026 New York State personal income tax rate schedules have been revised to reflect certain income tax rate reductions enacted under Chapter 59 of the Laws of 2025 (Part A). Accordingly, effective for payrolls made on or after January 1, 2026, employers must use the revised withholding tax tables and methods in this publication…"

The one place the withholding table doesn't help: past $1,077,550. New York's own Method II tables stop there and hand off to a separate "Method III — Top Income Tax Rates" method built for oversized one-time bonus payments. Method III's rates — 10.45%, 11.10%, 11.70% — are not the real marginal rate: they run a flat +0.80 percentage points above the published 9.65% / 10.30% / 10.90% top rates, a deliberate over-withholding margin for exactly the kind of payment that method is meant for (conceptually the same kind of payroll markup as California's 1.1× EDD figure, confirmed by checking that Method III's own number does not agree with Method II's last row, while the true top rates do once continued as ordinary brackets). The three rows above $1,077,550 in this model use the confirmed-true rates, not Method III's — moot in practice, since this tool's audience never approaches seven-figure taxable income in a single year.

PART FIVE · IMPLEMENTATIONThe one benefit narrow enough to expose a real bug

Georgia's flat rate couldn't test whether the withdrawal engine assumed linearity. Virginia's phase-out could, and did. California's surtax kink needed no new mechanism at all — proof the fix built for Virginia generalized cleanly. New York broke that streak: it is the first benefit narrow enough that pooling every kind of withdrawal into one number, which had worked for three states running, quietly over-granted it.

New York's $20,000 exclusion covers pension, annuity, and IRA/401(k) withdrawals — not brokerage-account gains, and not non-medical HSA withdrawals. But the withdrawal engine's gross-up solver had always priced every kind of draw through a single shared figure, because Georgia's exclusion happens to cover gains too and Virginia and California don't distinguish income types at all. Under this tool's default withdrawal order — taxable brokerage accounts drawn before tax-deferred ones — a brokerage sale's gain share was quietly riding into the very same pooled figure the tax-deferred exclusion was being sized against:

// yearlyProjection.ts — widened from a single `draws` figure to a typed breakdown
(breakdown) => computeStateTax(stateRules, { ...stateInputsBase,
              taxDeferredWithdrawals: breakdown.taxDeferred,
              brokerageGains: breakdown.brokerageGains,
              hsaNonMedicalWithdrawals: breakdown.hsaNonMedical }).tax - initialStateTax
Measured effect after the fix

Across a 60-seed sweep of the default plan, New York now leaks $0.68 per run (worst single year −$0.25) — indistinguishable from Georgia's $0.52, Virginia's $0.74, or California's $0.68. Georgia, Virginia, and California all pass their existing tests unchanged under the wider, type-aware gross-up: nothing about their formulas depends on which income type a dollar came from, which is exactly why the widening is additive and not a rewrite.

What's new in the schema, and what isn't

New York needed no new top-level field on the rules type at all — its recapture lives entirely inside the ordinary graduated_by_status bracket rows California already introduced, and its retirement benefit is one more member of the existing retirementBenefit union, alongside Georgia's exclusion and Virginia's age deduction. What New York did need was a change outside that schema: Pension gained an isGovernment flag, StateTaxInputs.pensions split into governmentPensionIncome and privatePensionIncome, and the gross-up's stateTaxOnDraws callback grew from a single number into the typed breakdown shown above.

What is deliberately not modeled

  • NYC and Yonkers local income tax — New York is the only modeled state with a local income tax layered on top of the state one, and this tool computes neither city's. Disclosed in the Assumptions panel.
  • The $1,000 dependent exemption and the blind exemption — retirees rarely have dependents, the same reasoning as Georgia's and California's unmodeled dependent exemptions.
  • The continuation brackets above $1,077,550 are derived from the true published top rates rather than directly quoted (Part Three) — irrelevant in practice, since this tool's audience never approaches that income level.
  • The 59½ threshold is modeled as age 60 (Part Two) — a half-year more conservative than the statute, in the same direction as every other age-based shortcut this project takes.
