Georgia state tax for retirees

Why Georgia is unusually generous to retirees over 65 — unusually harsh to those who retire early — and how this simulator turns that into numbers.

Georgia taxes income at one flat rate, but almost none of a typical 65-year-old retiree's income ever reaches that rate. Two provisions do the work: Social Security is completely exempt, and a large slice of everything else is excluded by age. The catch is that the age exclusion doesn't exist at all before 62 — which is exactly when early retirees are living off their portfolios.

The whole model in one line

GA tax = 4.99% × max(0, [federal AGI − taxable Social Security] − retirement exclusion − standard deduction)

PART ONE · THE TAXHow income becomes taxable in Georgia

Georgia starts from your federal adjusted gross income and then subtracts three things. Each subtraction is a different mechanism, and they stack:

Subtraction 1 — Social Security is fully exempt

Georgia does not tax Social Security at all. Note the subtlety: the simulator subtracts the portion that was federally taxable, because that is the only part that ever entered federal AGI in the first place. Georgia also insists that Social Security is left out of the retirement-exclusion arithmetic entirely — it cannot be used to soak up exclusion room.

Subtraction 2 — the retirement income exclusion, the big one

This is Georgia's signature retiree benefit. It is per person, it steps up with age, and it is capped by each person's own eligible income.

A married couple can reach 2× the amount — but each spouse must qualify on their own, against their own income. Two 66-year-olds can shelter up to $130,000 between them; a 66-year-old married to a 60-year-old can shelter $65,000, because the younger spouse's tier is still zero.

Which income counts as "eligible"?

The exclusion applies to specific categories, not to income generally. In this simulator's terms:

Subtraction 3 — the standard deduction

$15,000 for a single filer, $30,000 for a married couple (TY2026). Unlike the federal deduction there is no age-65 addition under Georgia's flat-tax structure, and unlike the federal deduction it is not indexed to inflation — it only moves when the legislature moves it.

PART TWO · THE SHAPEWhy age 62 is a cliff edge

Because the exclusion is zero until 62, jumps to $35,000, then jumps again at 65, a Georgia retiree's state tax bill has a distinctive shape: front-loaded into the earliest years and falling to nothing thereafter. This is the single most important thing the model captures, and no flat "add 3% for Georgia" adjustment can reproduce it.

The trap

Georgia's state bill lands hardest in precisely the years a "tax-smart" withdrawal strategy targets. That strategy draws from tax-deferred accounts up to the federal standard-deduction floor, on the reasoning that those dollars are tax-free. In Georgia before 62 they are not: the federal floor is far larger than Georgia's $15,000 deduction, and there is no exclusion yet to cover the difference. The draw is usually still worth making — it shrinks future RMDs — but it is not free, and this app no longer describes it as though it were.

PART FOUR · IMPLEMENTATIONHow the simulator computes it

The tax rules above are the interesting part. This section is a short tour of where they live in the code, for anyone reading the engine.

Constants live in JSON, read by two languages

Every Georgia number sits in src/lib/calculations/stateTaxRules.json with a link to the enacted bill text or the IT-511 instruction booklet. That file is deliberately JSON rather than TypeScript so the independent Python verifier, scripts/verify_plan.py, reads the same constants instead of re-declaring them. One file, two readers, no hand-kept sync — the formulas stay separately implemented, so the verifier still catches a wrong formula while human review against the primary source catches a wrong number.

The marginal rate is measured, not derived

To draw enough from an account to cover the state tax the draw itself causes, the engine needs Georgia's rate on the next dollar. That is not simply "4.99% once you're past the deduction", because the exclusion is capped by eligible income: while exclusion room remains, each extra dollar of tax-deferred withdrawal creates its own exclusion and costs nothing at the margin — even for a household already paying Georgia tax on other income.

Rather than encode that reasoning twice, the engine asks the question it actually wants answered — it re-runs the real formula with a $1,000 probe withdrawal and measures the difference:

// stateMarginalRate() — the finite-difference trick
const base   = incomeTax(rules, inputs);
const bumped = incomeTax(rules, { ...inputs,
                 taxDeferredWithdrawals: inputs.taxDeferredWithdrawals + 1000 });

return (bumped - base) / 1000;   // → 0, or 0.0499, or blended

This gets the exclusion growth, the deduction shield, and correct blending across a threshold the household happens to be sitting just under — all from the one formula that is already the source of truth. It will also handle Virginia's phase-out band, where the marginal rate doubles, without any new code.

Simplifications, stated plainly

  • Frozen contingent escalators — overstates Georgia tax, as described above.
  • Couples' accounts are pooled, so a withdrawal cannot be attributed to one spouse. Georgia requires each spouse to qualify separately, so applying the combined 2× cap to pooled income over-excludes a couple whose retirement income is lopsided.
  • The $5,000 earned-income sublimit is not doubled for couples, because the app models only one part-time income — there is no second earner to claim it.
  • Non-medical HSA withdrawals get no exclusion, the conservative reading.
  • State deductions are never inflated — correct, since Georgia's isn't indexed. This is a deliberate asymmetry with the federal deduction, which is.
  • Not modeled at all: the dependent exemption, the military retirement exclusion, and any state-level credits.
Verification

Georgia's arithmetic is re-derived independently in Python and cross-checked on exported runs — 854 of 854 checks pass across a single-filer and a married-filing-jointly scenario, both carrying genuinely non-zero Georgia tax. The engine also reproduces the published "zero tax bill" couple exactly on the Georgia side, pinned by a regression test.
