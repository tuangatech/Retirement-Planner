Retirement Planner · Tax Model Explainer

California state tax for retirees

California gives retirees no age-based benefit at all — but its personal exemption is a credit, not a deduction, its brackets vary by filing status, and a 1% surtax above $1,000,000 does not double for joint filers. None of that is hand-wavable into "add a few points," and none of it needed a single new mechanism in the engine to get right.

Georgia excludes a large slice of retirement income by age. Virginia gives a means-tested age deduction. California gives neither — a 401(k) withdrawal at 70 is taxed exactly like one at 60. Social Security is still fully exempt, as in every state this tool models. What makes California's engine work interesting is not a retiree benefit; it is three structural quirks that no other modeled state has, each of which forces a genuine design decision.

The whole model in one line

CA tax = max(0, brackets([federal AGI − taxable Social Security] − standard deduction) − exemption credit) + 1% × max(0, taxable − $1,000,000)

PART ONE · THE TAXA credit, not a deduction — and it comes off the tax

California starts exactly like Georgia and Virginia: federal AGI, minus the Social Security that was federally taxable, minus a standard deduction. There the family resemblance ends — there is no retirement exclusion or age deduction to subtract, so the next step is straight to the bracket schedule. The twist arrives after that: California's personal and senior exemptions are credits subtracted from the computed tax, not deductions subtracted from taxable income. Every other modeled state does the latter; California is the only one that does the former.

The brackets vary by filing status

Unlike Virginia, whose single four-bracket schedule applies to every filer, California runs two separate schedules. The lower seven brackets double exactly for joint filers; the top two do not — a genuine feature of the statute, not an approximation:

Retirement income does not get a preferential rate, and neither do capital gains: a brokerage gain is taxed identically to a 401(k) withdrawal, exactly as in Virginia and unlike the federal system's 0/15/20% long-term rates.

The personal exemption credit: $168 single, $336 married, +$168 more per spouse 65+. Statutorily it is a fixed dollar amount ($52 in 1987) inflation-adjusted every year by the same California-CPI factor that adjusts the brackets — R&TC §17054. The senior addition uses the identical base and indexing clause, so by statute it must equal the base credit in any given year; it just stacks per qualifying spouse.

PART TWO · THE SHAPEA credit that steps down, and a line that doesn't double

Two effects give California a genuinely different shape from Virginia's — one much smaller, one much sharper.

The credit phases out in $6 steps, not smoothly

Above $252,203 of AGI (single) or $504,411 (married), the exemption credit shrinks $6 — or $12 for joint filers — for every $2,500 increment of AGI over the threshold, floored at $0. This looks like Virginia's means test but behaves completely differently: Virginia's age deduction dies continuously, one dollar at a time, which is what doubles the marginal rate across its $12,000 band. California's credit only drops at $2,500 boundaries, so between crossings the marginal rate is whatever the bracket already says — untouched. Crossing a boundary adds a one-time $6 blip to the bill, not an elevated rate on everything around it:

At most $168–$336 is at stake, reachable only above a quarter-million dollars of retirement income — this app's audience essentially never gets there. It is implemented anyway, because the cost was small: the withdrawal engine already re-runs a state's whole formula at every candidate draw (Part Four), so a step function is no harder for it than a smooth curve.

The million-dollar line does not double for joint filers

California's Behavioral Health Services Tax (Prop 63 — renamed from the Mental Health Services Tax by Prop 1 in 2024) adds a flat 1% on top of the bracket tax for taxable income above $1,000,000. FTB's own 2026 Form 540-ES instructions describe it as a single subtraction on the household's combined taxable income, with no married-filer adjustment:

"Behavioral Health Services Tax (previously Mental Health Services Tax) – If your taxable income … is more than $1,000,000, complete the worksheet below. … Tax rate – 1%."

Because the $1,000,000 is a household figure regardless of filing status, two people who would each owe nothing filing singly can owe real money the moment they marry and file jointly on the same combined income:

Rare, but real

This app's audience is retirees drawing down savings, not million-dollar earners — the surtax essentially never fires at typical withdrawal levels. It is implemented in full anyway, for the same reason as the credit phase-out: once the engine re-runs the state's real formula at every candidate draw, an edge case costs nothing extra to get right, and "we modeled California's tax" should not come with an asterisk for the top of the distribution.

PART THREE · WHY THESE ARE 2025 NUMBERSCalifornia can't know its own current year

Georgia's and Virginia's schedules are set by statute years in advance — HB 463 already states Georgia's TY2027 exclusion, Virginia's 2030 deduction cliff is written into current law today. California cannot do this. Its brackets, standard deduction, and exemption credit are all re-indexed every year to the California Consumer Price Index, but the statute (R&TC §17041(h)(1)) measures that change using CPI data through June of the same tax year being indexed — so the true TY2026 numbers cannot exist until partway through 2026 itself, past when a real return would even use them.

FTB's own 2026 Form 540-ES instructions confirm this in as many words. Estimating 2026 tax, the worksheet tells taxpayers to reach for numbers from the year before:

"Tax. Figure your tax on the amount on line 3 using the 2025 tax table for Form 540 or Form 540NR." — and, two lines later — "Residents: Enter the exemption credit amount from the 2025 instructions for Form 540."

This simulator does exactly what FTB's own instructions do: it holds California's constants at the last closed, fully confirmed tax year — 2025 — rather than publishing a provisional guess of a number that legally cannot exist yet. The standard deduction ($5,706 / $11,412) is independently confirmed by two separate FTB/EDD documents; the bracket dollar thresholds needed one extra step.

How the bracket thresholds were recovered. ftb.ca.gov blocks automated access, so this model's brackets come from a different official source: California's 2026 payroll withholding schedule (EDD), whose "Method B" tables intentionally publish rates at exactly 1.1× the real statutory rate — a long-standing CA withholding markup. Dividing every published rate by 1.1 recovers precisely 1% / 2% / 4% / 6% / 8% / 9.3% / 10.3% / 11.3% / 12.3%, with the dollar thresholds unaffected. Checking exactly where the withholding table inserts an extra breakpoint for married filers — at $1,000,000, not at double the single top bracket — independently confirmed the surtax's non-doubling before the FTB instructions above made it explicit in words.

PART FOUR · IMPLEMENTATIONThe design built for Virginia paid for itself again

Georgia's flat rate could not test whether the withdrawal engine assumed linearity. Virginia's phase-out could and did — the fix was to stop computing a state rate at all and hand the withdrawal solver the state's whole formula as a function, re-evaluated at whatever draw it is currently testing. California needed no new mechanism to benefit from that fix.

A withdrawal large enough to cross the $1,000,000 surtax line is exactly the case a flat rate mis-sizes — the same shape of bug as Virginia's band, just from a kink instead of a phase-out:

// yearlyProjection.ts — unchanged since Virginia; California passes the same shape of function
(draws) => computeStateTax(stateRules, { ...stateInputsBase,
              taxDeferredWithdrawals: draws }).tax - initialStateTax
Measured effect

Across a 60-seed sweep of the default plan, California leaks $0.68 per run (worst single year −$0.26) — indistinguishable from Georgia's $0.52, Virginia's $0.74, or a no-income-tax state's $0.45. The state gross-up is exact for all three income-taxing states, not merely for the one it was built to fix.

What's new in the schema, and what isn't

California needed three additions to the rules type, none of them touching Georgia's or Virginia's existing fields: a graduated_by_status rate variant (brackets keyed by filing status), an exemptionCredit field (a credit with its own phase-out, applied after tax rather than before), and a surtax field (a flat rate above a threshold). California's retirementBenefit stays undefined — the field exists for Georgia's exclusion and Virginia's age deduction, and California proves the schema's discriminated union was the right call by simply never populating it.

What is deliberately not modeled

  • The exemption credit's own high-income phase-out is implemented (Part Two) — but the underlying dollar thresholds ($252,203 / $504,411) and the $6/$12-per-$2,500 mechanism are sourced from tax-preparation publications, not an independently pulled quote of R&TC §17054's phase-out subsection. Flagged for a follow-up primary-source check; the financial exposure is at most $168–$336.
  • The dependent exemption credit and the blind exemption — retirees rarely have dependents, the same reasoning as Georgia's unmodeled dependent exemption.
  • No local income tax exists in California, so unlike New York (NYC, Yonkers) there is nothing to add.
  • Brackets, the standard deduction, and the exemption credit are TY2025, not TY2026 — a structural consequence of Part Three, not an oversight. Re-verify once FTB's actual TY2026 Form 540 booklet closes out, not before.
